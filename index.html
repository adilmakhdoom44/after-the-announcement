After the Announcement

Telling People You Are Separating

Published 20 November 2026

At some point you have to start telling people, and most of the advice about separation skips this part entirely. It goes straight from the decision to the paperwork, as if the months of explaining it to colleagues, neighbors and your child's teacher happen on their own.

They do not, and for a lot of people this is the part that drains the most energy. Not because any single conversation is hard, but because there are forty of them and they all arrive while you are already tired.

Here is a way to handle it that costs less.

Decide the sentence before you need it

The single most useful thing is one prepared sentence that you can say without thinking.

Something like: we have separated, it was a joint decision, and the children are our priority. Or simply: we separated in the spring, we are doing all right, thank you for asking.

Write it, say it out loud a few times, and then use it everywhere. The same words for the neighbor, the dentist, the parent at the school gate.

The reason this helps is not that it sounds polished. It is that without a prepared sentence, every conversation becomes a decision about how much to say, made in the moment, usually while being caught off guard. That is what exhausts people.

Your sentence does not have to be the truth in full. It has to be true and short. Those are different things, and you are allowed the second one.

Three circles, three different answers

It helps to think about who gets what, in advance.

The inner circle is a handful of people who get the real version. Close friends, a sibling, whoever you actually lean on. Keep it small. The urge to assemble a large support group is understandable, and a large group means repeating the worst details many times.

The middle circle is everyone who needs to know because it affects them practically. Your manager, the school, the children's activities, anyone who handles your money or your post. They need facts, not feelings. We have separated, here are the new contact details, here is who collects on Tuesdays.

The outer circle is everyone else, and they get the one sentence. Neighbours, colleagues you do not work closely with, the extended family who will hear it anyway.

Most of the strain in this period comes from accidentally giving outer circle people the inner circle version, usually because they asked at the wrong moment and you were too tired to filter.

The school is not optional

Tell the school early, and tell them plainly. Not because they need the story, but because children often show it there before they show it at home.

What is useful to them: that the household has changed, who is authorized to collect, where reports and letters should be sent, and that both parents want to be kept informed.

Ask specifically for duplicate communication. Many schools default to one contact. If you do not ask, you can find out months later that you have been missing half the information, and from the outside it looks like disengagement rather than an admin setting.

Keep it factual with them. A school office is not a neutral audience, and anything characterized as a complaint about the other parent travels further than you would like.

What the children hear

Children get a different conversation again, and the thing to protect is simple. They should never be the ones telling people.

That means not sending them into a family gathering where nobody has been told. It means telling their close friends' parents so the news does not arrive at a sleepover. It means not asking them to keep any part of it secret, because secrecy is a weight they will carry and it teaches them the situation is shameful.

It also means not asking them what the other household has said about it. That turns an ordinary conversation into an intelligence exercise and children work out what is happening very quickly.

The people who react badly

Some reactions will land wrong. People will say the thing about staying together for the children, or ask what went wrong in a tone that suggests they already have a theory, or tell you about their cousin's terrible divorce at length.

Most of it is not malice. People are bad at this and reach for whatever they have.

Two phrases cover almost all of it. I am not going into the details, but thank you for asking. And: that is not something I want to discuss, how are you doing?

You will also lose some friendships, usually not the ones you expect. Couples you knew as a couple often drift, not because they chose a side but because they do not know how to hold both of you. It is worth knowing that in advance so you do not read it as a verdict on you.

Online, and the thing not to do

Do not announce it on social media while anything is contested. Not a statement, not a cryptic post, not a change of status with a long caption.

Anything posted publicly can be screenshotted and used, and the version of you that writes a post at eleven at night is not the version you want quoted. This is one of the few absolute rules in the whole business.

If you want to tell people online, a short factual note to a private group is enough. No detail about the other parent, no implications, nothing that reads as a case being made.

It gets easier, and not in a straight line

The first dozen times are the worst. After that the sentence comes out without the surge of adrenaline behind it, and at some point you notice you said it and barely registered.

Then something resets it. A form that asks for marital status. Running into someone who has been abroad for a year and has to be told from the start. Those are ordinary and they are not evidence of going backwards.

Where this gets harder

All of the above assumes the other parent is giving roughly the same account to the same people. Sometimes they are not.

If a different version is circulating, among family, at the school, among mutual friends, the instinct is to correct it everywhere. That rarely works. Correcting a story is how you end up in a public argument about a private thing, in front of the exact people you need on your side.

What works better is being consistent, boring and brief, and letting the difference between the two accounts do its own work over time. People notice who is campaigning.

The exception is anywhere that matters practically, like the school or anything official. There you correct the record once, in writing, factually, and keep a copy.

Deciding which of those applies to any given situation is exactly the kind of thing coaching for the practical side of a high conflict divorce is for. Not the legal question and not the emotional one. Whether to answer at all, and if so, in what words.

Start with the sentence. Write it today, before the next person asks.
